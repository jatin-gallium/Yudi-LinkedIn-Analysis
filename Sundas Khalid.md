I stopped going to the gym for 5 weeks and this is what happened 👇🏼

- I got 2 extra hours in my day to focus on work and other life stuff. 
- I lost appetite, had to remind myself to eat. 
- I lost 2 lbs of muscles and 2 lbs of fat
- sleep suffered - I woke up more often at night 
- more brain fog 
- forgot how good it feels to workout, and honestly didn't want to go back. 

I went back to the gym after 5 weeks yesterday and it felt amazing ✨ 

Through this experiment, I learned that yes, technically I can save 2 hours a day by skipping workouts and get more done.

But that’s the exact mistake I made early in my career 11 years ago, believing that being more productive meant working longer and sacrificing my physical health.

Taking care of your health isn’t time lost, it’s time invested.

Physical health sharpens your mental clarity, focus, and creativity, and in my case, it also teaches discipline. All critical elements to doing great work.

Productivity isn’t about time spent. It’s about how sharp you are during the time you have.

One of the best piece of career advice I have ever received 👇🏼

Is to surround yourself with people who support and inspire you. 

Throughout my career, I have been lucky to be surrounded by people who genuinely care about me as a person and my career goals and aspirations. 

However, I noticed my circle becoming smaller when I picked up content creation as a hobby along with a full-time job. 

There are a very few people who know what it takes, so consequently there are a very few people who are good candidates to be part of the circle. 

Not everyone gets it, which could leave you feeling like the odd one out ❌ but you are not odd; you are just unique. 

So a few years ago I made it my goal to go out out of my way and find such people who get it. I cold message them, coordinated virtual meetings and slowly built a network of people I could lean on. 

I met Linda Haviv through social media. We call ourselves the OG creators because we are 😂😅 

Jokes aside, Linda and I have so much in common. We both work full-time in big tech, create tech content as a hobby, entered tech through unconventional paths, have kids, and crazy busy lives with similar values, goals and aspirations ⚡️bonus: I think we are both funny so it’s always a good time when we are together. 

Last month I needed some career advice that only a very few people could give me and of course I talked to Linda. Linda not only gave me the best advice but also made me feel confident in my decision 👏🏼 

As you grow in your career, your circle of people will become smaller. But you don’t need a lot of people to begin with. You only need a few good ones 💡

If you don’t have such people in your circle, then make it a goal to go out of your way and find them. Send them a cold message, find something in common and figure out a way to connect and nurture it. I know it sounds complicated, but it’s really not ✨

Remember when “Prompt Engineer” was supposed to be the hottest job in AI?

3 years later, there are far fewer “Prompt Engineer” job openings than predicted.

Prompt engineering is a critical skill but not really a standalone job. It’s a skill of the future that is part of every job family.

When I first started using AI, I was that person typing “write me an email” and then wondering why the results were… trash 😂

That’s because:

❌ bad prompts = bad results
✅ good prompts = good results

But how do you actually learn to write good prompts?

You use prompting frameworks that at minimum defines objective, role, and context.

For example, here’s a basic framework I use often: ROSES 

Role: Define the AI’s expertise/identity
Objective: State the goal clearly
Scenario: Describe the situation
Expected Solution: Specify the desired outcome
Steps: List the process to follow

Another one I really like is CRAFTTI (Context, Role, Action, Format, Tracking, Target, Independence)

Pick one and be consistent

Should technical people managers code?

Steve Huynh post below sparked some great conversations — I have a different take from a data science and analytics perspective 👇🏽

In my experience, I don’t need manager to be pushing code.

What I do need is someone who can think deeply with me.

Someone who can look at a messy dataset or a vague product goal and say:

 💡 “Let’s clarify the question before we jump into modeling.”
 📉 “This metric might be misleading — what if we reframe it like this?”
 🧪 “Can we test that hypothesis with a causal inference approach instead of just correlation?”

I’ve worked in big tech for the last 10 years, and the most impactful leaders weren’t the ones shipping code, Google Colab or Jupyter notebooks.

They were the ones who could spar intellectually, help pressure-test the analysis, and connect our work to the bigger business or product strategy.

In the data world, context is everything.

And a technically sharp manager — who understands the science, the tradeoffs, and the storytelling — is way more valuable than someone trying to prove they can still code.

That said, staying close to the work does matter:
 💬 Ask sharp questions — about cohort definitions, model choices, and assumptions.
 🧠 Skim the SQL and Python, glance at the feature engineering, and know why your team chose one method over another.
 🔍 Read the dashboards — and understand how key metrics are calculated.

You don’t need to write the code — but you should understand it.

That’s how you earn trust and help your team think more clearly.

Not all ‘corporate politics’ are loud. Sometimes it looks like silence. 

It’s not always dramatic, loud Email thread—sometimes it’s subtle.

So how do you identify the subtle signs especially the signs that could hurt career trajectory silently? 

Chances are you will learn and identify these as you progress in your career. In order words, you will eventually learn it the hard way. 

But you don’t have to. Here are 3 red flags to watch out for: 

1. “Oh, I thought you were already looped in.”

If you are consistently finding things out after the fact, you’re already behind. I’ve learned to ask more questions and make sure I’m in the rooms where decisions start, not just where they get announced.

2. You’re present for the final decision, not the discussion.

By the time it’s a “final call,” influence is off the table. Try to get in early, even if it’s just to listen — because visibility matters.

3. Your ideas show up but without your name.

This one hurts. Document everything. Overcommunicate, share in writing, and speak up. Quiet work doesn’t always get remembered — and you can’t assume people will give credit.

Sundas is a person who I highly respect and I agree that during a highly competitive job market getting an internship is very hard. I have another idea that a CS/ECE student can do:

An ECE/CS student has three advantages and one differentiating advantage:
1. They have time (during summers and between classes)
2. They have access to a decent computer and know how to use it.
3. They know how to code - This is the differentiating advantage.

Instead of an unpaid internship, I would suggest they pick up an Open Source project and contribute whatever they can into it. Pick one in a domain they are in interested in and start contributing. Look through the bug-list and start fixing them. Think of features that you would like this OSS to have and send out a message to mailing-list or Discord and volunteer to implement them. If you find nothing, volunteer to add documentation. Almost all OSS I have come across can have more documentation/tutorials/FAQ guides. 

I see three advantages to this approach:

1. You will learn so much about the software development process, especially how to get into a complex ecosystem and make forward-progress. Newsflash: this is what you would be doing in like 99% of the companies. 

2. You get to work with like-minded folks who get to know you technically and have a positive connection. I cannot think about a better networking opportunity to showcase your expertise.

3. When you are applying for jobs, you have something to show. Your interview will go from, "Tell me about what you know about <Domain> " to "Oh, you implemented <X>, <Y>, <Z> features in <Domain>, can you talk about it?"

Believe me, having a patch/PR that you created to implement a feature in a domain that you are interviewing for will positively influence any interviewer.

FYI: This is what I did when I was starting out in 2002. the OSS I started on was GCC and BinUtils. I can tell you this has paid dividends over dividends. Working in OSS is a gift that keeps on compounding.

I am happy to give anyone give pointers on how to get started on OSS world.
