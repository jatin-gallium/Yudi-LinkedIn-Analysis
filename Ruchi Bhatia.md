Two years ago, I presented my data science project to Jensen Huang.

I was the youngest triple Kaggle grandmaster. I'd spoken at Google conferences. 

Today, I work at Amazon Web Services.

When I started, I had no roadmap. No mentor. 

Here's how I would break Into AI in 2025 if I had to start over -

> Week 1 — Python, Pandas, NumPy. You work with real messy data and learn to navigate it like you're already on the job.

> Week 2 — Your first ML project. You train models, evaluate them properly, and start understanding what's actually happening.

> Week 3 — Messy data. Missing values. Unbalanced classes. This is where you stop being a learner and start thinking like an engineer.

> Week 4 — Deployment. You take a model and turn it into something people can actually use. Clean, explainable, production-ready.

> Week 5 — GenAI foundations. LLMs, embeddings, pipelines. You learn how to connect your own data to AI in a way that actually works.

> Week 6 — Real GenAI apps. You chain prompts, add logic, integrate APIs. No more demos. Real tools.

> Week 7 — Agent workflows. Systems that reason, plan, use tools, and finish the job. This is where it starts feeling like real AI.

> Week 8 — Your capstone. You integrate ML, GenAI, and agents into one project you can walk through in interviews and actually be proud of.

If you had 8 weeks to focus, what would you build?

Everyone’s updating their resume.

Here’s what I shared (and what I wish I heard earlier):

1> Stop hiding behind your resume.
The students who stood out weren’t the ones with the fanciest profiles.
They were the ones who could explain what they built, what went wrong, and what they’d try next time.

2> Your projects tell your real story.
If you can walk someone through what you built, why it mattered, and what you learned, you’re already ahead.

3> LinkedIn doesn’t have to be a performance.
Share what you’re learning. Post small wins. Ask questions.
Use it as practice for talking about your work.

4> Networking is simpler than it looks.
Ask questions you actually care about. Remember what people tell you. Follow up. Most people don't, which is why it works.

5> Show up before you feel “ready.”
Don’t wait for a big title to share your ideas.
The more you talk about your work, the easier it gets.

Your career doesn’t start when someone “lets” you in.
It starts when you decide to show up and talk about what you’re building.

Attended NVIDIA GTC this week!

If you didn’t attend, here are the 7 things that actually matter from an AI perspective.

1> Training is no longer the main bottleneck
A few years ago, most teams struggled to train strong models. That has changed. Pretrained models are widely available and fine-tuning is more accessible. The harder problem now is getting consistent, reliable behavior once those models are deployed into real applications.

2> Inference is where systems break down
Running a model once is easy. Running it millions of times with low latency is not. As models move toward longer context and multi-step reasoning, response times increase and costs scale quickly. This is where most production issues are showing up today.

3> New hardware is being designed for inference, not just training
NVIDIA’s Rubin platform reflects this shift. Instead of optimizing only for large training runs, the focus is on serving models continuously. That includes handling longer sequences, higher memory usage, and sustained workloads rather than short bursts of compute.

4> Cost per token is now a real constraint
When a model is used in production, every generated token has a cost. Small inefficiencies get multiplied at scale. Teams are starting to evaluate models based on how much it costs to generate outputs, not just how good those outputs are.

5> Reasoning models change how systems behave
Models are no longer just generating short answers. They are producing longer chains of reasoning and calling tools. This increases compute, memory usage, and latency. It also makes evaluation harder because outputs are no longer single responses but sequences of steps.

6> Agents are being tested in real workflows
There was a lot of discussion around agents, but it was practical. Teams are experimenting with multi-step systems that plan and act. The open questions are around reliability, control, and evaluation. Many teams are still figuring out when agents actually improve outcomes.

7> The entire stack is now connected
Model design, inference performance, and application behavior are tightly linked. A change in model size or reasoning style affects latency and cost. Infrastructure decisions affect what models can be deployed. These tradeoffs are now part of everyday decision-making.

Last week, I was invited to speak at University of California, Berkeley about product management and one question kept coming up in different forms:

“How do I break into product if I’m still a student?”

My answer was simple:
Pick one real customer problem and take it all the way to a decision.

Most students try to prove they’re “PM material” by collecting surface-level signals: frameworks, interview prep, more ideas than execution.

What actually stands out is evidence that you can do the core PM job:
turn ambiguity into clarity.

Here’s a concrete way to do that:

1> Choose a narrow problem
Not “improve onboarding.”
Something like: “New users don’t activate because the first project setup takes too long.”

2> Talk to 5 people
Ask: what were you trying to do, where did you get stuck, what did you do instead?

3> Write the one-page decision
What’s the problem. Who it affects. What success means.
3 solution options. Tradeoffs. What you’d ship first and why.

4> Define a metric and a mechanism
One metric that reflects customer value.
One mechanism that makes improvement repeatable.

If you can walk into an interview and say:
“I found a real problem, synthesized customer signal, proposed tradeoffs, picked a direction, and defined how we’d measure it”
…you’re already ahead of most applicants.

That’s product.
And you don’t need a PM title to practice it.

If you’re a student trying to break into product, start here: pick one problem and show your thinking end-to-end.

The human average is 72%.

The thing I'd be investing my time in right now: understanding agentic AI architectures.

If that term is new to you, here's the short version: agentic AI is when a model doesn't just answer a question. It plans a sequence of actions, uses tools (APIs, databases, browsers, code interpreters), executes them step by step, checks its own work, and recovers when something fails.

This is the layer between "the model can do things" and "the model does the right things in the right order without breaking." 

And very few people understand it well yet.

If you want to get started, here's what I'd recommend:

1/ Read the ReAct paper (Yao et al., 2023). It's the foundation for how most agent frameworks work today. Short, readable, and it'll click fast.

2/ Build a simple agent. Use LangChain or CrewAI. Give it access to a search tool and a calculator. Ask it to answer a multi-step question. Watch how it plans, acts, and self-corrects. You'll learn more in an afternoon than from a week of reading.

3/ Study the failure modes. Agents break in predictable ways: infinite loops, wrong tool selection, hallucinated tool outputs, context window overflow. Understanding where they fail is more useful than understanding where they work.

4/ Follow what's shipping. Look at how Cursor, Devin, and OpenAI's operator work. These are real agentic products in production. Study their architecture decisions.

AI models are structurally incentivized to hallucinate.

Researchers showed that the way we train and evaluate language models, from next-word prediction all the way through accuracy benchmarks, systematically rewards confident guessing over admitting uncertainty. 

Even with perfectly accurate training data, facts that only appear once or twice in the dataset produce unavoidable errors.

And the metrics we use to measure model quality (accuracy scores, leaderboard rankings) actively penalize models that say "I don't know."

I think about this every time I see someone copy-paste an AI output without checking it. 

The real skill isn't getting an AI to produce an answer. 

It's knowing when the answer is wrong. 

And that requires exactly the kind of domain expertise that people keep saying AI will replace.

If you're using AI tools at work (and you should be), here's what I'd add to your workflow:

1/ Never trust a factual claim from an AI without checking the source. If it can't point you to a specific, verifiable source, treat the claim as a hypothesis, not a fact.

2/ Build your domain knowledge independently of AI. Read papers, talk to practitioners, work with real data. The deeper your independent understanding, the better you'll be at catching hallucinations.

3/ When an AI gives you a confident answer on something you're not sure about, ask it to argue the opposite position. If it flips just as confidently, that's your signal.

The models will keep getting better. But the incentive structure that produces hallucinations isn't going away. The people who understand that will use AI differently than the people who don't.
